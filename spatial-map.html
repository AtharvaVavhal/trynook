Spatial map

The building is the interface.

A floor plan that residents and staff both read from the same record.

Why a map

Beds are not interchangeable.

The bed by the window, the one beside the door, the upper bunk and the corner are very different to the person who will live there for a year. Most software reduces them to identical rows in a dropdown.

For residents

Understand a property in seconds and choose a bed by where it actually is, instead of needing a phone call to explain.

For staff

See occupancy as a shape: which rooms are full, where vacancies cluster and which floor is emptying.

One record, two audiences. The map a resident browses is generated from the inventory staff maintain. It isn’t a marketing illustration drawn separately and left to drift.

How it’s modelled

From property down to the bed.

  1. Property
  2. Building
  3. Floor
  4. Room
  5. Bed
LevelWhat it isPosition on the map
PropertyOne premises at one addressNone. A container
BuildingA structure within the property, often just oneNone, until a site plan exists
FloorA level, ordered so “above” and “below” mean somethingCarries the layout. The floor is what the map draws
RoomAn enclosed space with the label you already use, such as “203” or “Annexe 2”An outline on the floor’s layout
BedThe one thing that is allocated, labelled A, B, C, D within its roomIts own position inside the room

Identity and position are separate

“Room 203, Bed C” is a stable identifier that appears on receipts and agreements. Moving where a bed is drawn never changes which bed it is.

Layouts are versioned

When a property is re-planned, a new layout is drafted and published. A past resident’s record still shows the plan that was true when they lived there.

On a phone

Designed for a thumb.

Most prospective residents will be on a phone, and plenty of staff work from the corridor.

  • One-finger pan, pinch to zoom, tap to select
  • Details open in a bottom sheet, and the floor switcher sits within thumb reach
  • Bed targets are at least 44 px at the default zoom, or the map opens zoomed to one room at a time

For everyone

Accessible from the start.

A canvas of clickable shapes is unusable without a keyboard or a screen reader, so accessibility is a requirement, not a later polish.

Full keyboard use

Floors, then rooms, then beds in order. Arrow keys move between beds in a room, and a visible focus ring survives any status colour.

Beds say what they are

“Room 203, Bed C, window side, available, selected”, not just a letter. Changes are announced.

A list is a peer, not a fallback. Alongside the plan sits a structured list of floors, rooms and beds that does the same thing from the same data, released at the same time. If the only way to choose a bed were to click a shape, the product would exclude people for no reason.

For staff

Keep it current as part of the job.

  • Staff place room outlines and bed positions on a floor with an editor, using the same data residents see
  • Edits collect in a draft and go live only when published, so nobody is re-planned under a resident mid-edit
  • Publishing checks the geometry: no overlapping beds, no bed outside its room, no overlapping rooms
  • Beds without a position still exist and can be allocated. They’re simply flagged as not drawn yet

The rule that matters

The map is a view, never the authority.

A bed shown as free is a display computed when the page loaded. It is not a reservation and not a guarantee.

  1. Displayed availability. What the map shows right now.
  2. Selection. Your preference. It does not hold the bed.
  3. Confirmed allocation. The server re-checks availability inside a transaction before committing, and rejects conflicts.
